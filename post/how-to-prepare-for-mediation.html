How to prepare for mediation: a practical guide for clients

Most of what decides whether a mediation settles happens before anyone arrives. Here is what to do in the weeks beforehand, and how to use the day well.

By Gurprit Mattu, Consultant MediatorPublished 25 September 2025 · Updated 7 October 2026 · 7 min read

People often imagine that mediation is won or lost in the room. In practice, the parties who do best are the ones who arrive knowing what they need, what they could live with and what happens if they walk away without a deal. That thinking takes time, and it's hard to do well at four o'clock on the mediation day with an offer on the table.

This guide sets out the preparation we'd recommend to any client, whether you have solicitors acting for you or you're representing yourself.

Know what you're walking into

Mediation is a confidential, voluntary negotiation run by a neutral mediator. The mediator won't decide who is right, and they can't impose a result. Their job is to help each side understand the dispute more clearly and to find terms that both can sign.

A few points are worth fixing in your mind before the day:

  • Nothing is binding until a written settlement has been signed by or for each party.
  • What is said and offered during the mediation is without prejudice, so it can't be used in court later if the dispute doesn't settle.
  • You'll spend much of the day in your own room. The mediator moves between the parties, and joint sessions only happen when they're likely to help.
  • Anything you tell the mediator in private stays private unless you give permission to share it.

Knowing this changes how people behave. You can be candid with the mediator about weaknesses in your case, and you can test ideas without committing to them.

Get the paperwork in order

Gather the documents that actually matter: the contract or lease, the key correspondence, invoices, photographs, any expert or survey reports, and anything the other side has sent setting out their case. Put them in date order and remove duplicates. If you're dealing with a property dispute, the lease or title documents and plans usually sit at the centre of it.

Then write a one-page chronology. It forces you to separate what you know from what you assume, and it helps the mediator get up to speed quickly. If the mediator asks for a position statement, keep it short and measured. A position statement that reads like a closing speech tends to harden the other side rather than persuade them.

Your mediator will tell you what they'd like to see in advance. On our fixed-fee services there's a page limit for the papers, so pick the documents that carry the argument rather than sending everything.

Work out what you actually need

Most disputes are argued in terms of positions: a sum of money, a line on a plan, a demand for repairs. Underneath every position there are interests, the reasons that sum or that line matters to you. Settlements are usually built from interests, so it pays to be honest with yourself about yours. Common ones include:

  • Certainty and closure. Wanting the matter finished so you can stop thinking about it, stop paying for it and plan again.
  • Financial stability. Cash flow, timing of payments, tax, or the risk of an open-ended liability, which may matter more than the headline figure.
  • Reputation and relationships. Protecting your standing, keeping a working or neighbourly relationship, or avoiding publicity.
  • Recognition. An apology, an acknowledgement of what happened, or simply feeling that your side has been heard.
  • Control. Keeping the decision in your own hands rather than having an outcome imposed.
  • Fairness. Wanting a result that squares with your sense of what's right, or with your organisation's values.

Rank these for yourself. Then try to rank them for the other side. If you can see what they need, you may be able to offer something that costs you little and matters a lot to them.

Do the sums on not settling

This is the step people most often skip, and the one that makes the biggest difference. Before the day, work out what happens if there's no deal.

If the dispute goes to court, ask your solicitor for a realistic estimate of the legal costs from now to trial, and of how long it will take. Bear in mind that even a successful party in England and Wales rarely recovers every penny of its legal costs from the other side. Add the cost that never appears on an invoice: your own time, the time of staff or colleagues, and the strain of living with the dispute for another year or more.

Then think honestly about the risk of losing, or of winning on some points and not others. Nobody can promise you an outcome at trial, however strong your case looks. Put those figures together and you have a much clearer sense of what a settlement is worth to you, and where your walk-away point should sit.

Negotiators sometimes call this your best and worst alternative to a negotiated agreement. The label doesn't matter. What matters is that you compare any offer on the table with what you'd realistically get by walking away, not with the best result you can imagine.

Set a range, not a single number

Go in with three figures or outcomes in mind: what you'd ideally like, what you'd be content with, and the point beyond which you'd rather leave without a deal. Write them down and keep them to yourself, or share them only with your adviser and, if you choose, the mediator in private.

Hold that range lightly. You'll almost certainly learn something during the day that you didn't know before: a weakness in your case, a constraint on the other side, an option nobody had thought of. A good settlement often looks a little different from anything either side planned.

Decide who comes, and who decides

Someone attending for each party must have full authority to agree a settlement on the day. If a deal needs sign-off from an insurer, a lender, a board or a co-owner, tell the mediator early and make sure that person is available, ideally in the room or at least on the end of a phone.

It's fine to bring a supporter, a solicitor or another adviser, but everyone attending needs to be named to the mediator and the other side in advance. Think about whether each person will help you make a clear decision. Someone who is angrier than you are about the dispute may not.

Prepare with your solicitor, if you have one

If you have legal advisers, use them before the day as well as on it. Ask them to talk you through the strengths and weaknesses of your case in plain terms, and what a court might realistically award. Agree in advance who will speak for you in a joint session, if there is one. Many clients prefer their solicitor to open, and to add points themselves once they feel comfortable.

Ask your solicitor to prepare a draft settlement agreement, or a draft consent order if proceedings have started. Agreements are signed before everyone leaves, and having a template ready saves a great deal of time at the end of a long day.

If you don't have a solicitor, it is still sensible to take advice before the mediation on anything with legal, tax or property consequences.

Sort out the practical arrangements

For an in-person mediation, arrive early, bring the documents you'll want to refer to, and clear the rest of your day. Sessions can run beyond the booked time if the parties want to keep going, and you don't want a train to catch just as terms are coming together.

For an online mediation, test your connection, camera and microphone beforehand. Find a quiet, private room where you won't be overheard, and use headphones if others are in the house. Have your documents available on a second screen or on paper. Only people who have been notified to everyone may be in the room with you.

Tell the mediator what they need to know

Once your mediation is booked, complete our confidential pre-mediation information form. Use it to tell the mediator anything that will help them run the day: tensions between particular people, subjects you'd rather not raise in front of the other side, health issues, or a need for breaks, an interpreter or other adjustments.

If you don't feel able to sit in a joint session at all, say so. We can run the whole mediation in separate rooms.

On the day

  • Listen as well as speak. You'll hear the other side's case put in a way you may not have heard before. Even if you disagree, it tells you what they need.
  • Expect the first offers to be a long way apart. That's normal and says little about where things will end up.
  • Use the mediator. Ask them what they think the other side is really worried about, and test your ideas on them in private.
  • Take breaks. Long days are tiring, and tired people make poor decisions.
  • Don't sign anything you don't understand. Ask for time to read the agreement properly, and take advice on it if you can.

A short checklist

  1. Key documents gathered and a one-page chronology written.
  2. Your interests listed and ranked, and a guess at the other side's.
  3. Costs to trial, timescale and risks discussed with your adviser.
  4. Ideal, acceptable and walk-away positions noted.
  5. Decision-maker confirmed and attendees notified.
  6. Draft settlement terms prepared.
  7. Pre-mediation form completed and practical arrangements checked.
