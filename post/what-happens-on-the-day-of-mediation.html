What actually happens on the day of mediation

If you've never been to a mediation, the day can feel like a black box. Here is what happens, roughly in order, and what it tends to feel like from the inside.

Published 7 October 2026 · 5 min read

Most people arrive at their first mediation with a picture borrowed from television: two sides facing each other across a long table, trading arguments. The reality is quieter and, for most of the day, more private. Here's what to expect, from the moment you arrive to the moment you leave.

Before the day

By the time the mediation starts, a good deal has already happened. Everyone has signed the Mediation Agreement, which sets out the ground rules on confidentiality, fees and the mediator's role. The mediator has read the documents and any position statements, and has usually spoken to each side on the phone or by video. You'll have been asked to complete a confidential pre-mediation form, telling the mediator what matters to you and anything that would help them run the day.

That groundwork means the mediator arrives already understanding the dispute, and with a sense of where the difficulties lie.

Arriving, or logging in

Each side has its own room, whether that's a meeting room at the venue or a private breakout room online. You won't be expected to make small talk with the other side over coffee. The mediator will usually come to your room first to say hello, check who is there, confirm that someone present has authority to settle, and answer any last questions about the process.

It's a good moment to mention anything that has changed, or anything you're anxious about. If you'd rather not be in a joint session, tell the mediator now if you haven't already.

The opening

Some mediations begin with a short joint session. The mediator explains the ground rules and reminds everyone that the day is confidential and without prejudice. Each side may then be invited to say a few words about how they see the dispute, either personally or through their lawyer.

This isn't a court hearing. There's no cross-examination and nobody is there to decide who is right. A calm opening that acknowledges the other side's position, even while disagreeing with it, tends to help far more than a forceful one.

Many mediations skip the joint opening altogether, particularly where emotions are raw or the parties have already set out their positions in writing. Starting in separate rooms isn't a sign that things are going badly.

Private sessions

Most of the day is spent in private sessions. The mediator moves between the rooms, spending time with each side in turn. In your room, the mediator will ask questions, listen, and help you think about:

  • what really matters to you, beyond the headline figure;
  • how you think a judge would see the strengths and weaknesses of each side's case;
  • what continuing the dispute would cost you in money, time and stress;
  • what the other side might need in order to settle, and what you could offer.

What you say in a private session stays there unless you agree that the mediator can pass it on. That's what allows people to be frank about weaknesses and concerns they'd never admit across the table.

There will be stretches of waiting while the mediator is with the other side. It can feel as though nothing is happening, but it's usually where the most useful work is being done. Bring something to read, eat properly and use the time to talk things through with your advisers.

Offers and options

At some point, usually once each side feels heard, the conversation turns to proposals. The mediator carries offers between the rooms, and you decide exactly what may be passed on and how. The first offers are usually a long way apart. That's normal and tells you very little about where things will end up.

Not everything is about money. Proposals often include payment dates, works to be done, apologies, access arrangements, confidentiality terms or how the parties will deal with each other in future. Sometimes the mediator will suggest that the lawyers meet on their own, or that the decision-makers have a short conversation with the mediator present, if that seems likely to help.

The difficult middle

There often comes a point in the afternoon when the gap seems too wide and the whole exercise feels pointless. Many people who have been to a mediation will recognise it, and it's often the moment just before real movement.

The mediator may change approach here: testing assumptions more directly, asking each side to look again at the cost of not settling, or suggesting a different structure for a deal. Take a break if you need one. Tired people make poor decisions, and a short walk can change how an offer looks.

Reaching agreement

When the parties agree terms, we strongly encourage them to write those terms down straight away. The settlement agreement belongs to the parties, not the mediator: usually the lawyers draft it, often from a template prepared in advance, and the mediator stays on hand to help the parties work through any last points. If court proceedings are under way, the terms may be recorded in a consent order. The parties read the document carefully, ask any questions, and sign it before leaving.

Under our Mediation Agreement, no settlement is binding until it's in writing and signed by or for each party. That protects everyone from agreeing to something in the heat of the moment that doesn't survive being written down.

If you don't settle on the day

Not every mediation ends with a signed agreement. If yours doesn't, you leave with all your rights intact, and nothing said or offered can be used in court. In practice, the day is rarely wasted. The issues are often narrower, each side understands the other's position much better, and offers made during the day may remain on the table. If everyone agrees, the mediator can keep working with the parties afterwards.

How long it lasts

With us, a half day is four hours and a full day eight hours, running continuously with no deduction for lunch. If the parties want to keep going beyond the booked time, they can, at the additional hourly rate set out in our fees. Clear your diary for the whole period and a little beyond. Settlements are often finalised late in the day.

What to bring

  • Your key documents, on paper or a laptop.
  • A note of your ideal, acceptable and walk-away outcomes, for your eyes only.
  • Contact details for anyone who must approve a deal but isn't attending.
  • A charger, water and something to eat.
  • For online mediations, headphones and a private room.

How it feels

Mediation days are tiring. People are often surprised by how emotional they feel, even about a commercial dispute. That's normal. A good mediator will notice and give you room. But having it finished, on terms you chose, is worth a lot.
