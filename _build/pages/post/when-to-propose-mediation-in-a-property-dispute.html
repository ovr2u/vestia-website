Property disputes have a habit of costing more than they should. The documents are technical, experts are often needed on both sides, and the parties frequently have to carry on dealing with each other while the claim runs: landlord and tenant, freeholder and leaseholders, neighbours, co-owners. Those are exactly the conditions in which a well-timed mediation earns its fee.

This checklist is written for solicitors and in-house lawyers handling property claims in England and Wales. It covers the costs position, the courts' current approach to ADR, and the practical questions that decide whether a proposal to mediate will land.

The legal backdrop

Pre-action conduct

The Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct and Protocols treats litigation as a last resort and expects the parties to consider whether negotiation or some other form of ADR might resolve the dispute without proceedings. If a claim is issued, the court can look at how the parties behaved before it started, and failing to engage with ADR can count against a party on costs.

Costs consequences of refusing

Since Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust [2004] EWCA Civ 576, a party that unreasonably refuses to mediate has risked a costs penalty even if it wins. In Halsey the burden was on the unsuccessful party to show the refusal was unreasonable, and the Court of Appeal identified factors that bear on the question, including the nature of the dispute, the merits, whether other settlement methods had been tried, whether the costs of ADR would be disproportionately high, whether any delay in setting it up would be prejudicial, and whether ADR had a reasonable prospect of success.

In PGF II SA v OMFS Company 1 Ltd [2013] EWCA Civ 1288, the Court of Appeal went further: as a general rule, silence in the face of an invitation to participate in ADR is itself unreasonable, regardless of whether a refusal might have been justified.

Churchill and the power to order ADR

Halsey was long read as saying that the court could not compel unwilling parties to mediate. In Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council [2023] EWCA Civ 1416, a case about Japanese knotweed spreading from council land onto a neighbouring property, the Court of Appeal held that the courts can lawfully stay proceedings for, or order, the parties to engage in a non-court-based dispute resolution process. The order must not impair the very essence of the claimant's right to a judicial hearing, must pursue a legitimate aim and must be proportionate. The court declined to lay down fixed rules for when such an order should be made, leaving it to the judge's assessment of each case.

The 2024 rule changes

Amendments to the Civil Procedure Rules that came into force on 1 October 2024 put the position into the rules themselves. Promoting or using ADR is now part of the overriding objective (CPR 1.1). The court's case management powers include ordering the parties to engage in ADR (CPR 3.1). And when deciding costs, the court can take into account whether a party failed to comply with an order for ADR or unreasonably failed to engage in it (CPR 44.2).

The practical consequence is simple. A refusal to mediate now needs a reason you'd be comfortable reading aloud at a costs hearing, and a judge at the first case management conference may well ask why the parties haven't tried.

The checklist

None of these questions is decisive on its own. Taken together, they'll usually tell you whether now is the moment.

1. Do both sides know enough to value the claim?

Mediation works when each party can make an informed decision. In property claims that usually means the lease or title documents, the key correspondence and enough technical evidence to understand the issues: a dilapidations schedule and response, service charge accounts and supporting invoices, a surveyor's view on a boundary. It doesn't mean full disclosure and finalised expert reports. If the gap is narrow, consider exchanging specific documents before the mediation, or instructing a single joint expert, rather than waiting for the full litigation timetable to play out.

2. Will the parties have to deal with each other afterwards?

A landlord and tenant mid-way through a lease, a residents' management company and its leaseholders, neighbours, joint owners of a building: in all of these, the parties have to live with each other after the dispute ends. Litigation tends to damage that relationship further. Mediation gives them a chance to settle the immediate issue and agree how they'll deal with each other in future.

3. Are costs already out of proportion, or about to become so?

Look at the costs incurred so far and the estimate to trial against what's genuinely at stake. Service charge disputes over modest sums and boundary claims over a narrow strip of land are notorious for costs that dwarf the amount in issue. The points at which costs jump, typically before expert evidence, disclosure and trial preparation, are natural moments to propose mediation.

4. Is there a remedy a court can't give?

If the commercial answer involves a lease variation, a surrender and regrant, a payment plan, a schedule of works with a timetable, a licence for access, an agreed boundary line or anything else beyond damages, declarations and injunctions, mediation is the more natural forum. Settlement can include terms the court would never order.

5. Is there a deadline that mediation could help with?

Break dates, lease expiry, a sale or refinancing that depends on resolving the dispute, a development programme, remediation works that can't start until responsibility is agreed: a fixed date concentrates minds on both sides. A mediation booked a few weeks before that date often achieves more than months of correspondence.

6. Who needs to be in the room?

Identify early who can actually agree a deal. In property disputes that may include insurers, lenders, a parent company, a board, a residents' management company's directors, a managing agent or a local authority officer. If someone with a veto won't attend, the mediation may still be worth having, but plan for how their approval will be obtained on the day.

7. Is there a good reason to wait?

Sometimes there is. You may need an urgent injunction, or to issue protectively to stop time running out on a limitation period, and then seek a stay for mediation. A key document may be missing, or a point of law may need a ruling that goes beyond the parties. A reasoned "not yet, but after the experts have met" is very different from a flat refusal, and if you're on the receiving end of a proposal at the wrong moment, reply in those terms rather than saying no.

Making the proposal

A vague offer to "consider ADR at an appropriate stage" achieves little, and it won't protect your client on costs. A proposal is far more persuasive, and far more useful later, if it:

  • is made in an open letter, so it can be shown to the court on costs;
  • names two or three suitable mediators, with their availability;
  • proposes a format (online or in person, half or full day) and a timetable;
  • suggests how the mediator's fees will be shared;
  • explains briefly why mediation makes sense now, using the factors above;
  • invites the other side to propose alternatives if they disagree with any of it.

Offering a choice of mediators matters. A party that feels the mediator was picked by the other side is less likely to engage properly. Our fees are fixed per party and published, which removes one of the more common reasons for delay.

If the other side refuses or ignores you

  1. Chase in writing. Silence is generally unreasonable, but it's much easier to show that when you have followed up.
  2. Ask for reasons. If they say the claim is hopeless, or that they need more documents first, you now know what to address.
  3. Offer to meet their objection. A different mediator, an online half day to keep costs down, a later date after a specific step, or a short exchange of documents first.
  4. Raise it with the court. Address ADR fully in your directions questionnaire and costs budget, and if the refusal continues, consider asking the court to order the parties to engage in ADR or to stay the proceedings for it.
  5. Keep the record clean. Your correspondence may be read by a costs judge. Measured, specific letters will serve your client better than sharp ones.

Preparing your client

The quality of a client's decisions on the day depends on the preparation beforehand. Before the mediation:

  • give a realistic risk assessment in writing, including the range of likely outcomes at trial;
  • set out costs to date, estimated costs to trial and the likely irrecoverable element even if the client wins;
  • consider how any Part 36 or other offers already made fit with what may be proposed at the mediation;
  • confirm who has authority to settle, and arrange any third-party approvals in advance;
  • prepare a draft settlement agreement or consent order so terms can be recorded and signed on the day;
  • ask your client to complete our confidential pre-mediation information form, so the mediator understands what matters to them.
