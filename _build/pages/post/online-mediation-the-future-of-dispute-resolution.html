A few years ago, online mediation was something people agreed to reluctantly. Today it's routine, and for many disputes it is simply the better choice. It saves travel and venue costs, it makes it easier to get busy people in the same virtual building, and some parties find it far easier to negotiate from their own desk than across a corridor from someone they're in dispute with.

It isn't right for every case, though. This article explains how an online mediation works with us, where it shines and where we'd suggest meeting in person instead.

What online mediation is

An online mediation is the same process as one held in a room, run over a video platform. The same Mediation Agreement applies, with the same rules on confidentiality and without prejudice discussions. The mediator acts as host, and each party has its own private breakout room. The mediator moves between those rooms, and brings people together in a joint room only when that's likely to help.

If the parties reach agreement, the settlement is drafted on screen, circulated and signed electronically before everyone logs off. Our Mediation Agreement allows electronic signatures, so there's no need to wait for paper copies.

How the day runs

Before the day

Everyone signs the Mediation Agreement and sends the mediator their documents and, if asked, a short position statement. The mediator will usually have a preliminary call with each side. That's a good moment to raise any worries about the technology, about who will be attending, or about sitting in a joint session.

Joining

You'll receive a link in advance. Join a little early so there's time to sort out sound or camera problems. The mediator will check who is present on each side, because only people notified to everyone may attend or be in the room with you.

During the day

Most of the time you'll be in your own breakout room with your advisers, while the mediator spends time with each party in turn. There will be periods when the mediator is with the other side and you're waiting. That's normal, and it's usually a sign that work is being done. Keep your camera and microphone on when the mediator is with you, and feel free to switch them off during breaks.

Documents can be shared on screen or by email. Offers are carried by the mediator in the same way as in person, and you decide what the mediator may pass on.

Reaching agreement

Once terms are agreed, the written settlement is prepared, usually by the parties' lawyers. If you don't have a lawyer, talk to the mediator beforehand about how the terms will be written up. Everyone reads the agreement, signs electronically and receives a copy. Nothing is binding until that's done.

When online works well

  • The parties are far apart. Cross-border disputes and cases with people in different parts of the country are much easier to schedule online.
  • Feelings are running high. Some people negotiate more calmly when they don't have to share a building with the other side.
  • Costs need to stay proportionate. There's no venue to hire and no travel, which matters most on smaller claims.
  • Time is tight. Without a venue to book, an online mediation is usually the quickest to arrange.
  • Someone has access needs. Joining from home can make it easier for people with mobility difficulties or health conditions to take part, and breaks are simpler to manage.

When in person may be better

  • Many parties are involved. Large multi-party mediations often benefit from informal conversations between lawyers in the corridor, which are harder to replicate online.
  • A participant isn't comfortable with technology. If someone will spend the day worrying about the connection, they won't be concentrating on the negotiation.
  • The relationship matters a great deal. In family businesses and long-standing partnerships, sitting together for part of the day can help in a way that video doesn't.
  • Someone needs support in person. A vulnerable party may do better with their adviser or supporter physically beside them.

A hybrid mediation is also possible, with some participants in a room and others joining by video. It needs more planning, so tell us early if you'd like that option.

Keeping it confidential on screen

Confidentiality works the same way online, but it depends on everyone taking a few simple precautions:

  • Join from a private room with the door closed, and use headphones if anyone else is nearby.
  • Don't have anyone in the room, or listening in, who hasn't been notified to the mediator and the other side.
  • Don't record any part of the mediation. The Mediation Agreement prohibits recordings without everyone's written consent.
  • Close other applications and turn off notifications so documents don't pop up on a shared screen.

A quick checklist for participants

  1. Test the link, camera and microphone the day before.
  2. Use a laptop or desktop rather than a phone if you can, and keep a charger nearby.
  3. Have your documents on paper or on a second screen.
  4. Keep your phone on, in case the connection drops and the mediator needs to call you.
  5. Clear your diary for the whole booked period, and a little beyond.
