It usually starts with something small. A new fence goes up a little further over than the old one. A hedge is cut back hard. A neighbour's builder puts a footing where you thought your garden was. Before long there are solicitors' letters, a surveyor or two, and a dispute about a strip of land that might be a few centimetres wide.

Judges have recognised for a long time how badly these cases go. In a 1999 House of Lords case, Alan Wibberley Building Ltd v Insley, Lord Hoffmann described boundary disputes as a particularly painful form of litigation, in which feelings run high and disproportionate amounts of money are spent. Very little has changed since.

This article explains why boundary disputes escalate, why the paperwork rarely gives a clear answer, and why mediation is usually a far better way to resolve them.

Why boundary disputes get out of hand

A boundary dispute is never just about land. It's about your home, your privacy and your sense of being treated fairly by the people next door. Those feelings don't fade while the case goes on, because you see the fence, and your neighbour, every day. Each small incident adds to the grievance.

The money adds up quickly too. A contested boundary claim often needs surveyors on both sides, research into old conveyances and plans, site visits, aerial photographs and witness evidence about where features used to be. If the case goes to trial, the loser will usually be ordered to pay a large part of the winner's legal costs as well as their own. Even the winner is unlikely to recover everything.

Why the Land Registry plan rarely settles it

Many people assume the answer is on the title plan from HM Land Registry. Usually it isn't. Under the Land Registration Act 2002, the boundaries shown on most title plans are "general boundaries". The plan shows roughly where the boundary runs, but it doesn't fix the exact line, and at the scale of most plans a pen line can cover a strip of land wider than the one in dispute.

Where the exact line matters, it is usually worked out from the original conveyance or transfer that first divided the land, read alongside the physical features on the ground at the time: walls, hedges, fences and ditches. Those features may have moved, rotted or been replaced since. Sometimes one owner has used land as their own for many years, which can raise questions of adverse possession, and the rules on that differ depending on whether the land is registered.

The result is that two careful, honest people can look at the same documents and reach different conclusions. That's why surveyors' reports so often disagree, and why the outcome at trial can be hard to predict.

What going to court involves

A boundary claim is usually brought in the County Court. Alternatively, an owner can apply to HM Land Registry to have the exact line recorded as a "determined boundary", supported by a detailed plan. If the neighbour objects and the objection can't be resolved, the application is referred to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to decide.

Either way, you can expect expert evidence, disclosure of documents, witness statements and a hearing, often many months after the dispute began. And at the end, the judge decides one thing: where the line is. The court won't decide who rebuilds the fence and when, how the hedge will be maintained, whether your neighbour can come onto your land to repair their wall, or how the two households will get on afterwards. Those questions are often what the dispute was really about.

There's also the plain fact that one of you will lose, and you'll still be living next door to each other.

What mediation can do instead

In mediation, you and your neighbour decide the outcome together, with the help of a neutral mediator who understands property law. Because you write your own agreement, it can cover far more than a court could. A settlement might include:

  • an agreed boundary line, marked on a surveyor's plan and signed by both owners;
  • an application to HM Land Registry to record that line, if you want it on the register;
  • who will put up or pay for a new fence or wall, to what specification and by when;
  • who maintains which hedge, wall or ditch in future;
  • a right to come onto the neighbouring land for maintenance or repairs, with notice;
  • arrangements for drainage, planting or overhanging trees;
  • a contribution to each other's costs, or an agreement that each side bears its own;
  • an apology, or an agreed way of dealing with each other from now on.

The mediation is confidential and without prejudice. If it doesn't settle, nothing said can be used in court and you keep all your rights. If it does settle, the agreement is written up and signed before you leave, so there's no room for misunderstanding afterwards.

When to mediate

As early as you can. The best time is before positions harden and before each side has paid for its own surveyor's report. If expert evidence is needed, consider instructing a single surveyor jointly, with both of you agreeing the questions and sharing the cost. A jointly instructed surveyor's view often gives the mediation a firm footing.

If proceedings have already started, it's not too late. Mediation can take place at any stage, and the courts of England and Wales expect parties to consider it seriously. Refusing to mediate without a good reason can lead to a costs penalty later, and the court can order parties to try it.

A note on party walls and high hedges

Some neighbour disputes have their own statutory routes. Building work affecting a party wall or close to a boundary is governed by the Party Wall etc. Act 1996, which has its own procedure involving surveyors. Complaints about tall evergreen hedges can be made to the local council. Mediation can still help in both situations, especially with the relationship and with issues that fall outside those processes.

If you're in a boundary dispute now

  • Don't take matters into your own hands. Moving a fence, removing a hedge or putting up a new structure while the boundary is disputed usually makes things worse, legally and personally.
  • Keep a record. Date-stamped photographs of the boundary features, and a short note of what happened and when.
  • Get your documents. Obtain official copies of both registers and title plans from HM Land Registry, and any old deeds you hold.
  • Keep communication calm and in writing. Avoid discussing the dispute on social media or with other neighbours.
  • Propose mediation. A short, polite letter suggesting mediation, offering to share the cost and leaving the choice of format open is often all it takes.
