People come to mediation with very different expectations. Some picture a courtroom without the wigs. Others expect something closer to counselling. Neither is quite right. This article explains what mediation is, what we mean by a client-centred approach, and how you can shape the process so it works for you.

What mediation is, and what it isn't

Mediation is a voluntary, confidential negotiation, guided by a neutral mediator. The parties decide the outcome. The mediator doesn't judge who is right, doesn't give legal advice to either side and can't impose a result.

That makes it different from the other ways disputes get resolved:

  • Court produces a binding decision by a judge, in public, after a process that can take a long time and cost a great deal. The judge can only grant the remedies the law allows.
  • Arbitration is private, but it is still a decision imposed by a third party.
  • Negotiation through solicitors can work well, but letters going back and forth often harden positions, and each side only ever sees the other's case through correspondence.

In mediation, nothing is binding until the parties sign a written settlement. Everything said and offered along the way is without prejudice, so it can't be used against anyone in court if the dispute doesn't settle. That freedom is what lets people speak openly and try out ideas.

What client-centred means in practice

We don't run every mediation the same way. Before the day, the mediator speaks to each side to understand what's going on and what would help. That conversation shapes the day. It might affect:

  • The format. Online, in person, or a mix of the two.
  • The rooms. Whether there's a joint session at the start, later on, or not at all. Plenty of mediations happen entirely in separate rooms.
  • The pace. Longer breaks, shorter sessions, or a mediation split over two days if that's what someone needs.
  • Who attends. A supporter, an interpreter or a family member, provided everyone knows in advance.
  • Language. Some of our mediators can conduct mediations in languages other than English.

None of this changes the mediator's neutrality. It simply recognises that people negotiate better when they feel safe, are heard and aren't exhausted.

Facilitative and evaluative mediation

Mediators work along a spectrum, and it helps to know where yours sits.

A facilitative mediator concentrates on the process. They help each side explain what matters, ask questions that open up options, and carry proposals between the rooms. They won't tell you what they think of the merits of your case.

An evaluative mediator will, if the parties want it, go further and test each side's legal position: pointing out risks, asking how a particular argument would hold up in front of a judge, or questioning an expectation that seems unrealistic. This can be useful when the parties are a long way apart because of very different views of the law.

Many mediations move between the two during the day. What matters is that you know what to expect and can ask for what's useful. If you'd prefer the mediator to stay purely facilitative, or would value a frank view of the risks, tell us when you book.

Interests, not just positions

In most disputes, each side starts with a position: pay this sum, move that fence, withdraw that claim. Positions are usually incompatible, which is why the dispute exists. Underneath them sit interests, which are often far less opposed than they look.

Take a simple, typical example. Two neighbours argue about a high hedge. One wants it cut to two metres; the other refuses. Talking it through, it turns out the first neighbour's real concern is light in a back room, and the second's is privacy for a garden where their children play. Once those interests are on the table, there are options neither position allowed for: reducing the hedge on one stretch only, replacing part of it with a fence, or agreeing a cutting timetable.

A good part of the mediator's job is to help each side see its own interests clearly, and to see the other's.

Outcomes a court can't order

Because the parties write their own agreement, mediation can produce settlements that a court couldn't. A court can only grant the remedies the law provides, such as damages, a declaration or an injunction. A mediated settlement can deal with money and a great deal more, for example:

  • an apology, or an agreed statement acknowledging what happened;
  • a payment plan that fits someone's cash flow;
  • an agreed reference or announcement in an employment dispute;
  • arrangements for the future, such as access for repairs, maintenance responsibilities or how future disagreements will be handled;
  • a timetable for works, a sale or a handover.

When mediation may not be the right step

Mediation suits most disputes, but not all of them, and not always at the first attempt. It may not be the right step yet if:

  • someone needs urgent protection from the court, such as an injunction;
  • there are safeguarding concerns that make a negotiated process unsafe;
  • a party needs a binding ruling on a point of law that goes beyond their own case;
  • one side doesn't yet have the information it needs to make a decision.

Even then, mediation may well be useful later. Where there has been harm, a structured restorative process can sometimes help, provided there are no safeguarding concerns.
