About mediation

What is mediation?

Mediation is a confidential, voluntary process in which an independent mediator helps the people in a dispute negotiate their own settlement. The mediator doesn't decide who is right and can't impose an outcome. Their job is to help each side understand the other, test its own position and find terms everyone can sign.

Is mediation legally binding?

The process itself isn't. Anyone can withdraw at any time, and nothing said during the day commits you to anything. A settlement only becomes legally binding once it has been written down and signed by or on behalf of each party.

We normally draft and sign the settlement agreement before the end of the mediation day, so nobody leaves with a handshake that can unravel overnight. If court proceedings are under way, the parties' solicitors often record the terms in a consent order.

Is it confidential?

Yes. Everything connected with the mediation is private and confidential, including the fact that it is happening, unless the parties agree otherwise. It is also without prejudice, which means what is said or offered can't be used in court later if the dispute doesn't settle.

If you tell the mediator something in a private session, they won't pass it on without your permission. There are narrow exceptions, for example where the law requires disclosure or someone is at risk of harm. These are set out in our Mediation Agreement.

Will the mediator give me legal advice?

No. The mediator stays neutral and does not advise either side. Our mediators are experienced litigators, so they understand the legal issues and can help you think about risk, but you should take your own legal advice on whether a settlement is right for you.

Do I need a solicitor?

You don't have to have one, and plenty of people mediate without. Where there's a legal claim, it makes sense to get advice before the day and to have any settlement checked, especially if it involves property, money held in trust or a court case. You're welcome to bring a solicitor or another adviser with you.

What kinds of dispute do you mediate?

Our deepest experience is in property: commercial and residential landlord and tenant, service charges, building safety, boundaries and neighbour disputes. We also mediate commercial and civil claims, probate and inheritance disputes, employment matters, private family disputes and community conflict. See our property specialisms and other specialisms.

When is the right time to mediate?

Usually earlier than people think. Mediating before proceedings saves the most in costs, but it can work at any stage, including after a claim has been issued or shortly before trial. The important thing is that each side has enough information to make a decision. If key documents or an expert's view are missing, it may be worth getting those first.

What if the other side won't mediate?

Mediation is voluntary, but the courts of England and Wales expect parties to consider it seriously. Unreasonably refusing to mediate, or ignoring an invitation altogether, can lead to a costs penalty later on. Since the Court of Appeal's decision in Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council in 2023, it has been clear that the courts can also order parties to take part in mediation or another form of dispute resolution.

Put your proposal in writing and explain why you think mediation could help. A free call with one of our mediators can help you decide how to frame it.

Costs and paying

How much does mediation cost?

Our fees are fixed and quoted per party. For a dispute worth £20,000 to £100,000, a half day is £800 + VAT and a full day £1,200 + VAT per party. For £100,000 to £1 million, it is £1,000 + VAT and £1,500 + VAT. Smaller disputes can use an abridged mediation, typically from £600 + VAT per party, and larger or non-monetary disputes are priced to suit. The full table is on our fees page.

Who pays?

Unless the parties agree otherwise, our fees are shared equally between them. The parties can agree a different split, for example one side paying the whole fee. Each party pays its own legal costs of the mediation unless the settlement says something different.

What does the fee include?

All preparation and administration time, including reading the papers and any preliminary meetings with the parties before the day. Travel is included unless the mediator needs to travel for more than five hours in total, and any such costs are agreed with you in advance. For in-person mediations, the parties book and pay for the venue.

What if the mediation runs over time?

If everyone wants to keep going beyond the booked half or full day, additional hours are charged at £150 + VAT per hour per party (£20,000 to £100,000 disputes) or £175 + VAT per hour per party (£100,000 to £1 million). These are invoiced after the mediation.

What if we need to cancel or postpone?

Once a date is agreed, cancelling or postponing costs 25% of the basic fee with more than seven working days' notice, 50% with three to seven working days' notice, and the full basic fee with fewer than three working days' notice, plus VAT. Free 15-minute calls can be moved with 24 hours' notice.

The mediation day

What happens on the day?

The mediator usually starts by meeting each side privately, then moves between you, with joint sessions only where they help. Offers are explored and refined until either terms are agreed and signed or it's clear that more time or information is needed. Our article What actually happens on the day of mediation walks through it step by step.

How long does it take?

A half day is four hours and a full day eight hours. The time runs continuously, with no deduction for lunch. Larger disputes can be booked over more than one day.

Will I have to sit in the same room as the other side?

Not unless you want to. Much of the day happens in separate rooms, with the mediator moving between them. If you don't feel able to take part in a joint session at all, tell us beforehand and we'll plan the day around that.

Online or in person?

Either, or a mix of the two. Online mediation runs on video with private breakout rooms for each side and works well for many disputes. Some people find it easier to settle face to face. We'll talk through the options with you.

Who should attend?

At least one person on each side must have full authority to agree a settlement on the day. If someone else has to approve a deal, such as an insurer or a board, the mediator and the other side need to know in advance. Everyone attending must be named beforehand, and you can bring a solicitor, adviser or supporter.

How should I prepare?

Gather the key documents, think about what really matters to you beyond the headline figure, and work out the outcome you'd accept as well as the one you'd like. Our guide How to prepare for mediation covers this in detail. Once your mediation is booked, please complete the confidential pre-mediation information form.

Can you make adjustments for my needs?

Yes. We can offer extra breaks or shorter sessions, work with interpreters (including British Sign Language), provide documents in other formats and consider venue access. Gurprit Mattu can mediate in Punjabi and Hindi, and Claudia Haisman-Green speaks German. Our accessibility statement has more detail.

After the mediation

What if we don't settle?

You keep all your existing rights, including the right to go to court, and nothing said in the mediation can be used there. The day is rarely wasted: the issues are often narrower and each side understands the other's position better. If everyone agrees, the mediator can keep helping after the day.

What happens to my documents afterwards?

Once the mediation and any follow-up work are finished, the mediator returns or destroys all documents received from the parties and permanently deletes mediation notes and confidential information. Our Privacy Policy explains what limited records we keep and why.
